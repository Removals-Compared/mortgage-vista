The buy-to-let (BTL) tax landscape has been steadily reshaped over the last decade, and the cumulative effect now genuinely changes whether and how to hold residential property. This is a practical summary of the rules in force today, with the numbers landlords need for their own decisions.

## Section 24: mortgage interest relief restriction

Section 24 of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2015 phased out the ability for individual landlords to deduct mortgage interest as an expense before calculating taxable rental profit. The restriction has been fully in effect since the 2020/21 tax year, but its impact has grown sharply as interest rates rose.

Today, individual landlords:

- Cannot deduct mortgage interest from rental income as an expense
- Instead receive a 20% basic-rate tax credit on the interest paid
- Are taxed on the gross rental profit at their marginal rate

The practical consequence: a higher-rate taxpayer landlord with a mortgage costing £8,000 a year on a property generating £15,000 in rent now pays tax on £15,000 (less other allowable expenses) and gets back only £1,600 as a credit. Pre-Section 24, they would have paid tax on £7,000.

For a basic-rate taxpayer the effect is broadly neutral. For higher and additional-rate landlords with significant gearing, it has turned what looked like profitable portfolios into break-even or loss-making ones.

## SDLT additional-property surcharge

The Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) surcharge on additional residential properties is currently 5%, applied on top of the standard rates. So a £300,000 second property attracts:

- Standard SDLT: £2,500 (2% of £125k-£250k = £2,500; 5% of £50k = £2,500; total £5,000)
- Plus 5% surcharge on the full £300,000 = £15,000
- **Total SDLT: £20,000**

For non-UK residents, an additional 2% layer applies. A non-resident buying that same £300,000 BTL pays £26,000 in SDLT.

## The case for limited-company SPVs

A Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) is typically a limited company set up specifically to hold rental property. It sidesteps Section 24 because corporation tax treats mortgage interest as a normal allowable expense. The numbers:

- Corporation tax on company profits: 19% up to £50,000, 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief in between
- Full mortgage interest deductibility against rental income
- Dividends drawn from the company taxed at the shareholder's dividend rate (8.75% basic, 33.75% higher, 39.35% additional, after the £500 allowance)

For higher-rate taxpayers building a portfolio, the SPV structure usually wins on tax even after factoring in the slightly higher mortgage rates lenders charge limited companies (typically 30-50 basis points above personal-name BTL).

Caveats worth flagging:

1. Transferring existing personally held property into a company is a sale for CGT and SDLT purposes. It isn't a paperwork exercise.
2. Company accounts and corporation tax returns add £600-£1,200 a year in compliance costs per company.
3. Extracting profits as dividends adds a second layer of personal tax. For landlords who want to reinvest rather than draw income, the SPV is more efficient than for those who want to live off the rent.

## Capital Gains Tax on disposal

CGT on residential property gains is now charged at:

- **18%** for gains within the basic-rate band
- **24%** for gains in the higher-rate band

The 24% rate (reduced from 28% in April 2024) applies to most landlord disposals. The annual CGT exempt amount is just £3,000.

Reporting deadline: any UK residential property gain must be reported and the tax paid within 60 days of completion via the HMRC Capital Gains on UK Property service. Missing this attracts penalties even if no tax is ultimately due.

## Making Tax Digital for landlords

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax Self Assessment (MTD ITSA) now applies to landlords with combined property and self-employment income above £30,000. From April 2027 the threshold drops to £20,000.

Affected landlords must:

- Keep digital records of income and expenses
- Submit quarterly updates through MTD-compatible software
- File a final declaration after the tax year end

Practical software options include FreeAgent, Hammock, Landlord Studio and Xero. Expect £10-£25 per month per landlord, depending on portfolio size.

## Impact on yields and the bigger picture

For a typical higher-rate landlord with a £200,000 BTL on a 75% LTV mortgage at 5.2% interest-only, generating £1,200 a month rent:

- Gross rental yield: 7.2%
- Mortgage interest: £7,800/year
- Maintenance, insurance, voids: ~£2,000
- Net pre-tax cash flow: ~£4,600
- After tax (including Section 24 effect): ~£1,800

That's a 0.9% net yield on equity. Capital growth needs to do the heavy lifting for the investment to make sense.

## Practical next steps

- If you hold property personally and you're a higher-rate taxpayer, run the SPV-comparison numbers with an accountant before your next purchase.
- Diary your MTD obligations now if your income exceeds the threshold.
- Review your property's EPC rating ahead of tightening minimum standards. Band C is the proposed floor for new tenancies from 2028.
