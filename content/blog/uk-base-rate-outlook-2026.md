The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) cut the base rate to 3.75% earlier this month, the latest in a sequence of measured reductions that has pulled UK borrowing costs down from the 5.25% peak that defined most of 2024. For anyone with a mortgage decision in the next year, the direction of travel matters more than the headline number, so it is worth being clear about what has actually happened and what the MPC is signalling for the rest of 2026.

## How we got here: from 5.25% to 3.75%

Rates began falling in August 2024 with a 25 basis-point cut. The pace has been deliberate rather than dramatic. Through 2025 the Committee delivered a further four reductions as services inflation cooled and pay growth softened. The most recent meeting confirmed a fifth cut of this cycle, taking Bank Rate to 3.75% with a 7-2 vote split.

The MPC has been explicit about what it is watching:

- Services CPI inflation, which is still running above the 2% target
- Private sector regular pay growth
- Spare capacity in the labour market
- Energy and goods price pass-through

Crucially, the Committee has used the word "gradual" in three consecutive minutes. That language is doing a lot of work — it tells markets not to expect back-to-back cuts even if a single data print disappoints.

## What the swap market is pricing

Two-year sterling swaps closed last week at around 3.62% and five-year swaps at roughly 3.55%. That mild inversion — five-year money cheaper than two-year — is why your broker can quote you a 5-year fix at a lower headline rate than a 2-year fix today. It does not mean five years of cuts are guaranteed; it means the market expects rates to bottom out somewhere around 3.0%-3.25% in late 2027 before drifting upward again.

Forward markets currently imply two further 25 basis-point cuts during 2026, most likely in August and November, though a single hot inflation print could remove one of those.

## Fix versus tracker: how the maths looks now

A typical 2-year fix at 75% LTV is sitting around 4.39%. A 2-year tracker at the same LTV is roughly Bank Rate + 0.65%, so 4.40% today. The two products are essentially identical in starting cost.

The tracker wins if Bank Rate falls by another 50 basis points within twelve months and stays there. The fix wins if cuts stall, or if you simply want certainty for budgeting reasons. In practice most borrowers I speak with want the fix — not because the maths is overwhelming, but because the mental cost of watching rates every month is not worth the £15-£25 monthly difference.

## If you are remortgaging in the next twelve months

Three practical points:

1. **Lock in early.** Most lenders will let you secure a new rate up to six months before your current deal ends. If rates fall further before completion, you can usually switch to the cheaper product without penalty. If rates rise, you keep the lower one. There is almost no downside.
2. **Run the product transfer numbers first.** Your existing lender's retention deal is the benchmark. A full remortgage to a new lender only makes sense if it beats that benchmark by enough to cover legal and valuation fees — typically a £400-£600 hurdle on a free-legal product, more if you are paying separately.
3. **Watch the SVR drift.** Standard Variable Rates have not fallen as fast as new business rates. If your fix ended six months ago and you are sitting on the SVR at 7%-plus, every month you delay costs real money — often £200-£400 on a £200,000 balance.

## What could change the picture

The biggest single risk to this outlook is a renewed energy shock or a sterling slide that pushes import inflation back up. Either could pause the cutting cycle. Conversely, a sharper-than-expected slowdown in employment would likely accelerate it.

## Practical next steps

- Check when your current deal ends and diary a reminder for six months out.
- Ask your broker for both a fix and a tracker quote so you can see the trade-off in pounds and pence.
- If you are on an SVR right now, get a product transfer illustration this week — not next month.
